Notes from the person signing the invoices.

Written by me, and posted when a decision actually gets made — not on a schedule, and not to keep a page busy. There is no lease yet. When there is one, you’ll read it here first.

Every entry below is stamped August 2026, because August 2026 is when this page went up. I’m not back-dating anything to make the project look older than it is.

August 2026

What a 60-guest baby shower actually costs here, itemised.

Nobody planning a shower gets handed the whole number. You get a room quote from one place, a per-tray price from another, a balloon person on Instagram, and somewhere around the third spreadsheet tab the total quietly becomes a surprise. So here is the arithmetic for a Saturday-morning shower for sixty, done in public, the way I’d want it done for me.

The room: our morning block runs $1,400 to $2,000 — that’s 10:00 to 3:30, set up, torn down and cleaned, with someone from us in the building. The food: your caterer, at whatever your family actually pays — call it $30 a head, so $1,800 for sixty. The dessert table is usually the centrepiece, so give it real money: call it $400. A balloon garland from us is $300 to $550 if you want it handled; a chafing set is $140 if your caterer isn’t hauling their own. Add 13% HST on our lines and you land somewhere between $4,100 and $5,300, all in, for a morning that didn’t borrow a hall built for someone’s wedding.

Two honest caveats. The room number is a band and not a quote, because there’s no lease yet — that story is further down this page. And the catering line is your number, not mine: I used $30 because it’s the middle of what families tell me they pay, and your aunty may beat it soundly. The point isn’t the exact total. The point is that you should be able to see it before anyone asks for a deposit — the long version, with the timeline, lives at baby and bridal showers.

August 2026

What a mehndi night needs from a room, and why halls say no.

A mehndi is not a dinner. It’s the loosest, loudest, most sat-on-the-floor night in the whole wedding calendar, and it asks four small things of a room: floor seating without a furniture negotiation, a corner with real light where the henna artist can work for four hours, food that arrives in waves rather than courses, and drums. Every one of those requests is cheap. Every one of them gets refused somewhere in Brampton every weekend.

Not out of malice — out of design. A banquet hall is built around a plated dinner: rounds of ten, a service corridor, a kitchen pushing three hundred covers on a clock. Floor cushions break the table map. A henna corner blocks a service lane. Trays arriving at nine upset a kitchen that plates at seven. The hall isn’t being difficult; it’s being itself. The mismatch is the building, which is why the answer is a different building.

So the mehndi is one of the nights we’re designing for by name — low seating on the add-on list, lighting that can drop to something warm, a caterer of your own because that’s the whole point, and an evening that runs to eleven with the dhol welcome. The full picture — the hour-by-hour, the honest money, what to ask a henna artist before you book her — is at mehndi and sangeet nights.

August 2026

Why 50 to 125, and not 300.

Every hall we ever rented was built for somebody else’s wedding. Eighty people in a room made for three hundred feels like a rehearsal: the empty places photograph louder than the full ones, and the DJ ends up playing to a dance floor sized for a crowd that was never coming.

The arithmetic runs the same way in the other direction. A room that has to fill three hundred places has to charge like a room for three hundred, and that is where mandatory catering and food minimums come from. Once the food is carrying the rent, the menu stops being about your family. I’m not going to solve that problem by getting bigger; the only honest fix is to stay small enough that the room pays for itself on the rental alone.

So the cap is the promise, not something we’re apologising for. Sizing a room to the guest list, instead of asking the guest list to grow into the room, is the whole decision, and nearly everything else on this site follows from it. If your list runs to three hundred, Brampton has halls that do that well. If it’s 50 to 125, that’s the room we’re building — and the longer version of this argument has the numbers in it.

August 2026

Why the caterer stays yours.

Every hall we rented made its money on food, and the food was worse for it. That isn’t a coincidence. When the room and the menu arrive on the same invoice, the menu gets designed to protect the invoice, and your mother’s opinion of the biryani stops being anybody’s problem.

Handing the catering back to you costs us the easiest margin in this business, and we’re giving it up deliberately. Book the room and bring whoever cooks for your people: the caterer your cousin swears by, the aunty who does the sweets, the restaurant that has been feeding your family since before you could drive. Any cuisine — halal, vegetarian, Caribbean, Filipino, whatever the day calls for. No in-house menu, no food minimums, no surcharge.

All we’ll ask is who’s cooking and when they need to get in, because that’s how a day runs on time. This is the rule I’d be likeliest to trade away when the numbers get tight, which is precisely why it’s written down in public — here, and again on the outside catering page, where the questions to ask any venue are listed out.

August 2026

Why the price is a band and not a number.

We haven’t signed a lease yet. That is the whole honest reason the packages are published as ranges instead of a price list. What a room costs to run depends on the building — the rent, the ceiling height, the parking, how many washrooms it already has. None of those are numbers I get to know before I sign.

There were two easy ways out of that and I didn’t take either. One is to print a single attractive figure now and quietly walk it back once the lease exists, which is a lie with a delay on it. The other is the price list that opens with the word “from”, which this whole category has taught people to read as “more than this, and we’ll tell you how much more on the phone”. Both work. Both would cost me the only thing I have before there’s a building.

So both ends get printed. The Space is $1,200–$1,500 Monday to Thursday and $1,600–$2,200 Friday to Sunday, and if we end up at the top of that band, you already knew the top of that band. Three things move the number: the day of the week, how long you need the room, and how finished you want it when you walk in. Nothing else. No seasonal surcharge, no per-head fee, no charge for serving what you actually eat.

The add-ons don’t depend on the room, so those stay exact to the dollar — overtime is $175 an hour, a chafing dish set of four is $140, and neither of those changes when a lease gets signed. The package bands are also 2027 dollars rather than today’s, because that’s the year you’d be standing in the room.

When we sign, the bands become a price. Founding families won’t feel that: their rate locks at the top of the published band for their day, so if the number moves, theirs doesn’t. Every band, package and add-on is on one page, with the 13% HST named beside it.

August 2026

What we’re looking for in a space.

We haven’t signed a lease. That’s the honest status, and it’s why the one photograph on this site is captioned as a feeling rather than as our room. What I can give you instead is the list I carry into every viewing, which is more useful than a description of somewhere we don’t have.

Room for 50 to 125 people to sit and eat without the place feeling thin at fifty. Somewhere a caterer can warm and plate — that one is on the requirements list for every space we look at. Step-free access and accessible washrooms, which are non-negotiable in anything we sign. Daylight, because a celebration that starts at ten in the morning shouldn’t feel like it starts at ten at night. A landlord who is genuinely relaxed about a different caterer every weekend and music running to eleven. And Brampton itself — not near Brampton.

When we sign, you’ll read it here first, and the founding families will see the place before anyone is allowed to call it finished.

August 2026

Listings don’t tell you where the caterer parks.

I’ve been walking through units. Nothing is signed and I have nothing to announce, so treat this as what it is: notes from the search, not news about a building. But the search has taught me more about what this room needs than the year of planning that came before it.

A commercial listing tells you the square footage, the rate per square foot, and whichever two adjectives the agent likes best. It does not tell you where a van can pull up on a Saturday morning, or how far that spot is from wherever a caterer would set up. It doesn’t tell you whether the washrooms are on the same level as the room. It doesn’t tell you what the neighbouring unit does at nine on a Friday night. Those are the questions the whole business runs on, and none of them are in the ad.

A viewing answers most of it in the first two minutes, if you stop walking and stand still. Sound is the one that keeps surprising me: an empty box and the same box with a hundred people and a speaker in it are different buildings, and you can hear which one you’re in by clapping once. I’ve started asking to be left alone in a space for a few minutes, which agents find odd and do anyway.

The other half of a viewing is the landlord, and that is not in the listing either. A different caterer in the building most weekends. Deliveries early. Music until eleven. Some landlords hear that and relax, and some hear it and start talking carefully about the other tenants. That answer decides more than anything on the drawings does, and it’s the reason a space can look right and still be wrong.

So: still looking, still nothing to report. I’d rather post that than post nothing and let the quiet do the lying for me.
