We kept renting rooms that were never going to be ours.

A room in Brampton for 50 to 125 guests, with your own caterer and nobody else’s menu, opening in 2027. Here’s why it exists.

A letter from Khushbu Dhahan, founder

Every celebration our family has ever thrown started the same way, with weeks of searching. Before every mehndi night, every baby shower, every milestone birthday, I went hunting across Brampton for a room that was beautiful, that let us bring our own food, and that didn’t cost more than the party happening inside it. I never found all three at one address.

The hunt always ended at the same two doors. A banquet hall built for three hundred, where eighty guests rattle around and an in-house menu quietly replaces the food your family actually eats. Or a community centre, cheap and honest about nothing but itself, where everybody dresses up anyway and politely agrees not to mention the room. For 50 to 125 guests, the middle was missing.

By day I deliver government programs — operations, budgets, compliance. Big programs, hard deadlines, and no tolerance for “it fell through.” I’m also a mom, which is its own kind of program delivery with considerably worse hours. At some point it stopped making sense to keep running the same search and losing it.

So I stopped waiting for somebody else to build the room, and started building it. The Laurel Room is that missing middle: 50 to 125 guests, any caterer you like, any cuisine, no minimums, and every price printed in public with the 13% HST named beside it. It is not open yet and we won’t pretend otherwise — what you’ll find here is the plan, the real numbers, and a journal of each decision as I make it.

If you’ve run the same search I have, I’d genuinely like to hear what you’re planning. I answer my own phone.

— Khushbu

A hall built for 300, or a community centre.

Those were the two options in a city of more than 700,000 people that celebrates constantly. For 50 to 125 guests, the middle was missing.

Brampton throws mehndi nights, nikkahs, aqiqahs, naming ceremonies, showers, Diwali and Eid parties, retirements, and birthdays that need a room and a sound system. Almost none of them are the size the city’s halls were built to hold.

The banquet hall is built for three hundred and priced for three hundred, and it makes its real money on food — which is exactly why the menu belongs to the hall and not to you. Book it for eighty and you have paid for a crowd that was never coming and eaten from a list your family didn’t choose. The community centre will happily let you bring your own caterer, and it looks precisely like what it is: the photographs come back with the fluorescent light still in them.

Between those two doors there was nothing for 50 to 125 guests, which is the size of nearly every celebration our own family ever threw. The mehndi the night before the wedding. The aqiqah. The shower. The sixtieth birthday where everyone in the room already knows everyone else.

We’re not claiming nobody in Brampton can host you. If your list runs to three hundred, this city has halls that do that properly. We’re saying that if your list is 50 to 125, you’ve been renting the wrong size for years and paying extra for the privilege.

Notes from the person signing the invoices.

The decisions and the reasoning behind them, written down as they get made, by me and by nobody hired to sound like me.

Why 50 to 125, and not 300.

Every hall we ever rented was built for somebody else’s wedding. Eighty people in a room made for three hundred feels like a rehearsal: the empty places photograph louder than the full ones, and the DJ ends up playing to a dance floor sized for a crowd that was never coming.

The arithmetic runs the same way in the other direction. A room that has to fill three hundred places has to charge like a room for three hundred, and that is where mandatory catering and food minimums come from. Once the food is carrying the rent, the menu stops being about your family. I’m not going to solve that problem by getting bigger; the only honest fix is to stay small enough that the room pays for itself on the rental alone.

So the cap is the promise, not something we’re apologising for. Sizing a room to the guest list, instead of asking the guest list to grow into the room, is the whole decision, and nearly everything else on this site follows from it. If your list runs to three hundred, Brampton has halls that do that well. If it’s 50 to 125, that’s the room we’re building.

Why the caterer stays yours.

Every hall we rented made its money on food, and the food was worse for it. That isn’t a coincidence. When the room and the menu arrive on the same invoice, the menu gets designed to protect the invoice, and your mother’s opinion of the biryani stops being anybody’s problem.

Handing the catering back to you costs us the easiest margin in this business, and we’re giving it up deliberately. Book the room and bring whoever cooks for your people: the caterer your cousin swears by, the aunty who does the sweets, the restaurant that has been feeding your family since before you could drive. Any cuisine — halal, vegetarian, Caribbean, Filipino, whatever the day calls for. No in-house menu, no food minimums, no surcharge.

All we’ll ask is who’s cooking and when they need to get in, because that’s how a day runs on time. This is the rule I’d be likeliest to trade away when the numbers get tight, which is precisely why it’s written down in public.

What we’re looking for in a space.

We haven’t signed a lease. That’s the honest status, and it’s why the one photograph on this site is captioned as a feeling rather than as our room. What I can give you instead is the list I carry into every viewing, which is more useful than a description of somewhere we don’t have.

Room for 50 to 125 people to sit and eat without the place feeling thin at fifty. Somewhere a caterer can warm and plate — that one is on the requirements list for every space we look at. Step-free access and accessible washrooms, which are non-negotiable in anything we sign. Daylight, because a celebration that starts at ten in the morning shouldn’t feel like it starts at ten at night. A landlord who is genuinely relaxed about a different caterer every weekend and music running to eleven. And Brampton itself — not near Brampton.

When we sign, you’ll read it here first, and the founding families will see the place before anyone is allowed to call it finished.
