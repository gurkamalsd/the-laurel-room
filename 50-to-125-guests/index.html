Home / 50 to 125 guests

A hundred seated guests need about 1,500 square feet. Nobody puts that on a website.

If you’ve been hunting for an event space for 100 guests around Toronto and getting back halls built for three hundred, this is the arithmetic those halls leave out — and how to test any room’s claimed number before you put money on it.

Planning figures the trade uses

Per seated guest
12–15 sq ft

Seats 8

4.5 sq ft

General rules of thumb from the rental and catering trade, not measurements of our room. We haven’t signed a lease, so we have no room to measure yet. That part is further down, stated plainly.

A chair pushed back takes as much floor as the guest sitting in it.

A banquet chair is about eighteen inches deep. Pull it back far enough for a grown adult in a sari or a suit to stand up, and you’re two feet clear of the table edge. Put another table behind that chair and its guests want the same two feet going the other way. Leave enough room for a server to walk between the two chair backs carrying a full tray and you want three feet, and closer to five if they have to pass while somebody is standing up to hug an aunt.

Run that outward from a 60-inch round and the table stops being five feet across. It becomes a circle roughly ten feet across, and inside that circle sit eight people. Ten feet across is about 78 square feet of floor, which is a shade under ten square feet each — before a single aisle has been drawn anywhere. Once the aisles are in, the planning figure most people in the trade work to lands between 12 and 15 square feet per seated guest. You can do it at 10. It feels like 10.

So a hundred seated guests is somewhere between 1,200 and 1,500 square feet of dining floor. That is the number before the dance floor, the buffet run, the head table, the cake table, the DJ and the bit of floor by the door where people stop to greet each other. Add those back and a hundred-guest dinner with dancing wants something closer to 2,000 square feet to sit in without anyone turning sideways to get past.

Why this page exists

Search for a small banquet hall for 100 people and you get rooms built for three hundred with a partition down the middle. Search for a venue for 80 guests in Brampton and you get the same rooms back again. The capacity every hall advertises is the largest number the building can hold, because that is the number that sells the largest bookings.

Why we went looking for the middle

Everything in the room that is not a table.

  • The dance floor About 4.5 square feet for each person actually dancing, and something like four guests in ten are on the floor at the peak of the night. For a hundred guests that is a floor around fifteen by fifteen. 180–225 sq ft
  • The buffet run An eight-foot table carries four chafing dishes. Allow three feet of queue on the guest side and three feet of working room behind it, and each table in the line claims a strip of floor about eight by eight. ~65 sq ft each
  • The head table Long, backed against something worth photographing, with clear floor in front of it so the photographer can stand far enough back to get everyone in one frame. 120–180 sq ft
  • The rest of it Sound table, cake table, gift table, a bar if you’re pouring, the coat rail, and the greeting knot that forms inside every door and never moves all evening. 150–300 sq ft

A hall that says 300 is not offering you 300 dinners.

The number on the website is often an occupant load, which is a fire-code figure for bodies in a room. It was never a seating plan.

Occupant load is calculated for people standing at an assembly, and the allowance per person is a fraction of what anyone would seat for dinner. Take the same building at the planning figures above: three hundred seated at 12 to 15 square feet each needs 3,600 to 4,500 square feet of dining floor, and then a dance floor and a food line for three hundred on top of it. Rooms that genuinely do that exist in Brampton and they are enormous.

Plenty of rooms advertising three hundred are quoting the standing figure. Ask for the seated number with a dance floor in the room, and it commonly comes back a third lower. That isn’t dishonesty so much as habit — the big number is the one that has always been on the sign. It just doesn’t describe your evening.

Five questions that turn a claimed capacity into a real one.

You can do this on the phone, before you drive anywhere, for any venue in the city including ours.

How many guests can you seat for dinner, with a dance floor and a buffet line in the room at the same time?

That is the only capacity number that matters, and it is almost never the number on the website. Everything below is a way of checking the answer you get.

  • What are the room’s dimensions in feet? Multiply them, then divide by thirteen. That is roughly your seated ceiling before anything else goes in the room. A venue that can’t give you a length and a width has not measured itself. Length × width
  • How many tables do you own, and what size? Eight 60-inch rounds seat sixty-four people, whatever the floor could theoretically take. Furniture caps capacity as often as square footage does, and rentals arrive at a cost. Count them
  • Where does the buffet go, and can we serve from both sides? One serving line moves somewhere between fifty and seventy-five people through in half an hour. A hundred guests down a single side is most of an hour of your evening spent queuing. One side or two
  • What else is in the building that night? If your room is a third of a larger hall, the other two thirds have their own DJ, their own start time and their own guests looking for the washroom. Who’s next door
  • Can I see a photograph of it set for a hundred? An empty room photographs beautifully and tells you nothing. A picture of the last hundred-guest dinner shows you the aisles, the sightline to the head table, and how close the nearest table sits to the speakers. Set, not empty

Sixty, a hundred and a hundred and twenty-five are three different rooms.

The headcount doesn’t only change the number of chairs. It changes the table plan, how long the food takes, whether a dance floor still fits, and how full the room reads in the photographs afterwards.

Sixty guests

Seven or eight rounds, or four long tables set end to end.

  • Roughly 750 to 900 square feet of dining floor
  • One serving line clears the room in about half an hour
  • A twelve-by-twelve floor holds the twenty-five who dance
  • A toast carries to the back without a microphone
  • Everyone can see everyone, which is the whole point of sixty

A hundred guests

Ten rounds of ten, or thirteen of eight. The floor is committed.

  • 1,200 to 1,500 square feet before the dance floor goes in
  • One serving line takes most of an hour; both sides halves it
  • A fifteen-by-fifteen floor for the forty who dance
  • A microphone stops being optional
  • One bartender per fifty to seventy-five guests if you’re pouring

A hundred and twenty-five

Thirteen to sixteen tables, and every foot of floor is spoken for.

  • 1,500 to 1,900 square feet of dining floor on its own
  • Two serving lines, or you lose an hour of the night to the queue
  • The dance floor and the tables start competing for the same feet
  • Aisle discipline is what keeps it from reading as a crush
  • Serving staff run $40/hr each, and a buffet this size usually takes two or three
What each size costs, both ends printed

The room band doesn’t move with your headcount. There is no per-head fee here — sixty guests and a hundred and twenty-five pay the same number for the same day.

Eighty people in a room for three hundred, and the way it goes wrong.

The partition is the standard answer to this. Air walls fold out of a pocket, the hall becomes three halls, and yours is the third one with eighty chairs in it. On the invoice you have bought the right size. In the room, five things stay the size of the original building.

None of this is a knock on big halls. If your list runs to three hundred, book the hall that was designed for three hundred and it will do the job properly. The failure only shows up when the room and the guest list are two sizes apart.

The same question underneath

A building sized for three hundred has to earn like a building sized for three hundred, and food is where halls earn. That is why the menu usually has to be theirs.

Why the caterer stays yours

  • The air wall is a curtain that cost money Operable partitions damp sound, they don’t stop it. The DJ two rooms over arrives as bass through the floor, your side turns up to compete, and both parties spend the evening a little louder than they meant to be. You hear them
  • The proportions were set for the whole hall Ceiling height, window placement and the doors were all drawn for the full room. Take a third of it and you get a ceiling too tall for its footprint, often no window at all, and one very long blank wall where the head table has to go. Wrong shape
  • The empty floor is in every wide shot A photographer standing at the back needs the frame to fill with people. In an oversized room it fills with floor instead, and the pictures come back reading half-attended even though nobody left early. The camera counts
  • Nobody starts on a dance floor that big Dancers cluster, always. A floor laid out for three hundred with forty people on it reads as forty people hanging back rather than forty people dancing, and the ones deciding whether to join can see that. It stays empty
  • You pay for the whole building anyway Rent, staff, insurance and a kitchen sized for three hundred sit in the overhead whether or not you filled the room. It gets recovered through the food minimum, which is charged per head against a menu you didn’t choose. Through the minimum

Our own number is a target we’re shopping for, not a room we’ve measured.

Everything above this line is general trade arithmetic, and it applies to every hall in Peel including the one we end up in. Here is the part about us. We have not signed a lease. There is no Laurel Room to measure yet, which is why this page carries no floor plan, no square footage and no seating chart. Anyone showing you those for a building they don’t hold is showing you a drawing.

What we do have is the test we take into every building we walk through. It has to seat a hundred and twenty-five for dinner with a dance floor and a buffet line in the room at the same time, using the figures on this page rather than the landlord’s. And it has to still look full at fifty. Those two conditions together have ruled out most of what we’ve seen so far: the small units can’t take 125, and the big ones can’t make 50 look like anything.

When we sign, the real numbers go on this page — the dimensions, the table count, what the room seats with the dance floor in and with it out, and what the buffet line does to the aisle. Until that day, 50 to 125 is a design target and we’ll keep calling it one. The decisions get written down as they happen in the journal.

Most 100-guest Saturday evenings here will land between $3,200 and $4,300 all in, HST included.

That is the room, the styling, the staff most people end up adding, and the tax. Your caterer bills you separately, because your caterer is yours. Both ends of every band are on the pricing page.
