Most venues that allow outside catering still charge you for it. We won’t.

Bring your own caterer to a Brampton room for 50 to 125 guests. No in-house menu, no approved list, no food minimum, and no surcharge for serving what you actually eat. Doors in 2027.

Bring who you like. The price of the room doesn’t move.

That’s the whole rule. The rest of this page is what that sentence is worth in money, and how to tell whether another venue means it.

No catering fee. No food and beverage minimum. No approved-vendor list. No surcharge for serving what you actually eat.

You pay for the room, the hours, and how finished it is when you walk in. Your caterer bills you separately, because your caterer is yours.

When people search for a self catering venue, or a venue with no food minimum, this is what they mean: they want to bring the food in and pay for it once. It sounds like a small ask. Across Brampton, Peel and Toronto it turns out to be the hardest single thing to find, and the answers you get are rarely a straight yes or no.

Four rungs of restriction, and only the top one costs nothing extra.

“Outside catering allowed” is a phrase four different businesses use to mean four different things. Work out which rung a venue is standing on before you fall in love with the photographs.

  • Mandatory in-house The venue cooks or nobody does. The menu is theirs, the per-head price is theirs, and the tasting is the only negotiation on offer. Honest, at least, in that nobody pretends otherwise. In-house only
  • Approved list only You may bring a caterer, as long as it is one of the four on their sheet. Getting onto that sheet usually costs the caterer a commission, and the commission reaches your invoice wearing a different name. Their list
  • Allowed, for a fee Bring anyone — and pay a catering fee, or a per-head surcharge, or a food minimum you buy your way out of. This is the most common rung and the most quietly expensive one, because the number arrives late. A fee
  • Genuinely open Any caterer you like, no fee for the privilege, no minimum, no list. The venue makes its money on the room and says so. Rarer than it should be, and the rung we are building on. Where we sit
What the room itself costs

Room bands run $1,200–$6,200 plus HST, depending on the day and how finished you want it.

Food is where the margin is. That’s the whole reason.

A hall that rents a room for a Saturday evening earns that number once. The same hall feeding 150 people at $40 to $60 a head earns several times more, on the same night, in the same building. The room is the doorway. Dinner is the business.

So the rules follow the money, and they aren’t villainy. Most of these buildings were bought on the strength of a food P&L. The mortgage was underwritten on plates, not hours. Asking a hall like that to wave your cousin’s caterer through the door is asking it to give up the reason it exists, and it won’t, and you shouldn’t expect it to.

What you can expect is to be told plainly. The part worth objecting to isn’t the fee — it’s finding out about the fee on page four of a contract, in March, after you have sent a deposit.

We’ve chosen the other model. We earn on the room, which means the room has to be worth what we ask for it, and there is no second income stream to hide a thin week in. That is a harder business. It is also the only one that lets us say yes to your caterer without doing arithmetic first.

Six questions to ask any venue, and what a dodge sounds like.

Ask these on the phone before you drive anywhere. Use them on us too — a venue that can’t answer in one sentence hasn’t decided yet, and will decide once it knows your budget.

  • Is there a catering fee, and what is it called on the invoice? It travels under several names: catering fee, kitchen fee, service charge, administration fee, outside-vendor fee. Ask for the dollar figure and ask whether it moves with headcount. A venue that answers instantly has thought about it. “It depends” means the number is still being chosen.
  • Is there a food and beverage minimum? A minimum is a fee in a smock. If a hall rents for $1,900 and carries a $7,000 food minimum, the hall costs $8,900 and you are not permitted to spend less. Ask what happens if you come in under it. Almost always you pay the difference regardless, which means the minimum is simply the price.
  • Is there a preferred-vendor list, and what does it cost to go outside it? Two follow-ups, both worth asking out loud. What does a caterer pay to be on that list — a flat fee, a percentage, or nothing? And what is the surcharge for bringing someone who isn’t on it? “You can, but it’s an extra $600 and they’d need our approval” is a no with better manners.
  • What kitchen access does the caterer actually get? “There’s a kitchen” covers everything from a full commercial line to a sink and a folding table. Ask what is in it, whether your caterer is permitted to turn any of it on, what time they can load in, and whether using it carries a separate charge. Get the answer in writing, because your caterer will ask you and you need to be right.
  • Who cleans up, and what counts as cleaning? Three different jobs get blurred here on purpose: clearing plates during service, hauling away what the kitchen generated, and cleaning the room afterwards. Ask which is inside the venue price, which lands on your caterer’s invoice as staffing, and which is quietly yours at one in the morning.
  • What insurance does the caterer need, and by when? Most venues want a certificate of commercial general liability naming the venue, plus a deadline for it. Ask for the coverage amount and the date. If a venue can’t tell you either, the requirement surfaces two weeks out and your caterer is the one scrambling for paperwork while also cooking for a hundred people.

What we’ll ask of your caterer: five things, none of them a fee.

These are the requirements we intend to write into the booking, published now so nothing arrives as a surprise later. Pass them to whoever is cooking and they’ll recognise every line.

Proof of insurance
Commercial general liability, with The Laurel Room named, sent to us before the date rather than the week of it. Any caterer working the GTA already carries this — asking for the certificate is routine, not a hurdle we invented.
Their own equipment
Chafers, warmers, transport, serving utensils, the lot. If renting a set of four chafing dishes from us beats hauling them across the city, that’s an add-on at $140. Serving staff are $40 an hour each if you’d rather not staff the tables yourself.
The rubbish leaves with them
Food waste, packaging, boxes and oil go out at the end of the night. Cleaning the room sits inside your package price and always will. Hauling away what the kitchen produced is your caterer’s job, and every working caterer already prices for it.
They work inside your block
Load-in, service and load-out all happen within the hours you booked. If your caterer wants two hours before doors, book the block that covers it — overtime runs $175 an hour and nobody enjoys paying it at midnight.
A name and a number, in advance
Tell us who is cooking and how to reach them. Not for approval — there is nothing to approve. So that when a van pulls up on the day, we know whose it is and can meet them.

You’ll notice what isn’t on that list: a description of the kitchen. We haven’t signed a lease, so we won’t describe a room we can’t stand in yet. What we can tell you is what we’re hunting for — prep space a caterer can genuinely work in, and a load-in route that doesn’t run through the middle of your party. The day the lease is signed we’ll publish exactly what we got, including the parts we didn’t.

And none of the above applies to a family cooking for itself. If the food is coming out of your own kitchens, say so early and we’ll work it through together — the rules for feeding your own people are not the rules for a business, and we’d rather understand your plan than hand you a form.

We haven’t worked with a single caterer in this room, so we won’t hand you a list.

There is no room yet. Which means no caterer has cooked in it, no service has run late in it, and nobody has watched a team clean up at one in the morning and formed an opinion worth passing on. Any list we published today would be restaurants the founder enjoys, dressed as a vetting that never happened.

There’s a second reason, and it’s the reason this page exists. Preferred lists have a way of turning into paid lists. The moment a name on our page is worth money to the person on it, the page stops being advice and starts being inventory. We’d rather never run a list than run that one.

So the honest position: ask around, ask your family, and ask the last venue who they saw do good work. Then tell us. If someone has fed a hundred of your people well — a caterer, a restaurant that does trays properly, the aunty whose sweets nobody has ever matched — send us the name. We’re keeping a private note of who Brampton families actually rehire, and by 2027 we’d like to answer that question with something real behind it.

Email hello@thelaurelroom.ca, or put the name in the message box when you tell us about your event. We won’t publish it, and nobody pays us to be on anything.

And why we stop at 125 guests rather than building for three hundred, which turns out to be the same argument as this one from a different angle. When you’re ready, the enquiry form takes about two minutes and we reply within one business day.

Founding families go first, and their number stops moving.

First pick of the calendar, Saturdays included, and an invitation to see the space before anyone’s allowed to call it finished.

If you joined the list when we were advertising 10% off, that still stands — we’re not moving the goalposts on anyone who signed up early.

One email a month at most, and only when there’s something worth showing you.

Or just ask

Khushbu answers her own phone. If your caterer has a question about access, timing or paperwork, put them on the line — that’s a five-minute conversation, and it’s better had now than in 2027.
